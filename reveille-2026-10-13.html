Building an Arsenal That Survives the Tenth Salvo

"America faces a well-defined industrial base problem. Whether it's a hypothetical Taiwan invasion wargame run two dozen times, the current Iranian conflict, or the Russo-Ukrainian War, the same result repeats: Magazines of key weapons such as cruise missile systems and interceptors empty within days, weeks, or months, and the current industrial base takes years to replenish them. Part of that shortfall results from the analog way in which missile structures are designed, tooled, and sourced, which locks the entire supply chain into rigid, design-specific schedules. The first salvo depends on platforms, positioning, and doctrine, all things controllable by militaries."

How Taiwan Activates Beijing's Chosen Fear

"The standard explanations for why Taiwan is so important to the Chinese Communist Party are familiar: territorial integrity, credibility of deterrence commitments, great power competition with the United States, the strategic value of the first island chain, and the semiconductor industry concentrated in TSMC's fabrication facilities. From the Branch
Bernhardi Breaks Loose

"In 1914, the standard German cavalry regiment consisted of four squadrons, each of 150 or so mounted combatants. When the unit fought on horseback, all of these soldiers went into battle. When, however, the regiment fought on foot, every fourth man held the horses of three comrades. Thus, a mounted regiment of 600 'sabers' became a foot-mobile force of 450 'carbines.' In the first book he published after the end of the World War... Friedrich von Bernhardi called for a massive increase (from four to ten) in the number of squadrons in each regiment and a substantial reduction (from 150 to 100 men) in the fighting strength of each squadron. This reform, Bernhardi argued, would give each cavalry regiment the ability to deploy 750 dismounted men, thus making it the rough equivalent of an infantry battalion. (Strange to say, the infantry battalion Bernhardi proposed in the same book consisted of four rifle companies, each of 150 men. Thus, it is quite possible that he believed that it took three part-time foot-soldiers to achieve the same effect on the battlefield as two full-time infantrymen.)"

— Bruce Ivar Gudmundsson, The Tactical Notebook · Read the full piece › [more · less · never again]
