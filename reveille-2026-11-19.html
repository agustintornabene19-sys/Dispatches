How Australian Women Lost the Law [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Helen Dale & Katherine Deves

Every liberal democracy has a weak branch: in these days of overmighty courts, international human rights instruments, and grasping executives, it's often the legislature. The US Founders feared a Congress with teeth and claws. Britain's Parliament is supposed to be sovereign, able to make or unmake any law. In neither country do things work that way. Australia, too, has a weak branch: the courts. Despite its written constitution and judicial review of legislative competence, its Benthamite Framers sheared the founding document of rights, arguing that rights come from states and were therefore subject to democratic bargaining. Governments were instituted among men not because man had rights, but because he had none. This gave the courts and their judges few interpretive bones on which to gnaw. If rights are to exist in Australia, parliament must pass them into law. And when rights are repealed, it's always and everywhere "a matter for parliament." Laws must not be manufactured by the judiciary.
