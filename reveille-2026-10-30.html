The Long Reads

What did the Mongols do for Eurasia? [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Alexander Morrison

Mackinder's claim that 'European Civilization' (which for him was western European ) had been forged in repeated clashes with nomadic peoples was clearly exaggerated – even the Mongol conquests only touched Europe's fringes. He correctly identified a phenomenon, however, that was of truly world-historical significance: what historians would now call the 'closing of the steppe'. Between the 1750s, when the Qianlong Emperor's forces defeated and destroyed the Zunghar Confederation, and the 1890s, when the Russian Empire completed its conquest of the western half of Central Asia, nomadic military power in Eurasia was extinguished.

Hitler’s ‘Flood the Zone’ Strategy [more · less · never again]

Lawfare · Benjamin Hett

What followed Hitler's appointment as chancellor was a kind of "flood the zone" strategy—an early election call, a blizzard of executive orders curtailing press freedom and giving Hitler's paramilitaries police powers, a crescendo of violence, then the shock of the Reichstag fire followed by thousands of arrests. The election of March 5 produced somewhat underwhelming results for the Nazis—contrary to their own expectations, they fell short of a majority—but at the end of the month they got their Enabling Act anyway.

Washington Irving’s American Ghost [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Michael Lucchese

There is a reason "Sleepy Hollow" remains so thoroughly anthologized and adapted over 200 years since its publication. It has both delightful spooks and humor aplenty, but it is also an early expression of American identity. Set in 1790, it is haunted by the bloody legacy of the American Revolution. The Hudson River Valley environs where it takes place were the site of some of the bloodiest battles in the war, including bitter backwoods fighting between Tory and Patriot neighbors divided over the cause. Ichabod Crane's flight from the Headless Horseman on Halloween night is more than a thrill ride; the story was Irving's way of exploring how the ghosts of history shape how we see the world around us.
