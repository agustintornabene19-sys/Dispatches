The Long Reads

Supporting Trump Killed Evangelical Political Influence [more · less · never again]

Mere Orthodoxy · Mere Orthodoxy

There is a trope that Evangelicals are and were highly individualistic and apolitical, but scholars like Richard Carwardine, Robert Elder, Anne Loveland, and others have successfully argued that this was not the case. Historically, Evangelicals committed themselves to Christianizing society through the regulation of vice. Historians have emphasized this especially among Northern Evangelicals in major urban centers in the Early American Republic, but even Evangelicals in the South threw their weight behind the temperance movement and other reforms and policies designed to regulate vice. Evangelicals regarded politics in particular as social, and not individualistic. Historically, and especially before the 1980s invasion of Evangelical politics by Southern Baptists, Evangelicals were institutionalists and not populists.

Houellebecq Saw This Coming [more · less · never again]

Compact · Louis Betty

Michel Houellebecq's novels are our age's most profound and devastating studies in what it means to be godless. Pascal wrote that "nothing more surely underlines an extreme weakness of mind than the failure to understand the unhappiness of someone without God." Houellebecq's body of work is, in some sense, simply the working out in fiction of this observation. The godlessness he depicts is not only personal and social but (as explored in Submission ) political and civilizational as well. Considerations like these make what is now happening to Houellebecq feel all the more revealing. The author is accused of making statements liable to "incite racial and religious hatred," a crime under French law, for claiming in a 2022 interview that native Frenchmen were arming themselves to resist the future Muslim domination of France.

Why ‘Yalta 2’ Is Exactly the Right Analogy for a Trump-Putin-Xi Summit [more · less · never again]

Lawfare · Catherine Katz

On its face, declaring Shenzhen "Yalta 2" is an awkward analogy. Yalta was a meeting of World War II allies, not adversaries—though the Soviets would soon become such. As the Wall Street Journal Editorial Board recalls, Yalta is also colloquially understood as the moment the three Allied leaders carved up the world into spheres of influence, though that is not exactly the case. This idea originates in the " Percentages Agreement " drawn up between British Prime Minister Winston Churchill and Stalin while scribbling on a scrap of paper in Moscow in 1944, not at Yalta. And yet, at a deeper, far more concerning level, Yalta may accidentally turn out to be exactly the right analogy for a summit among Trump, Putin, and Xi in November, particularly amid lapses in intelligence sharing between the U.S. and Ukraine and Trump's decision to lift some sanctions on Russia , clear signals that the White House's well of support for Ukraine may soon be running dry.
