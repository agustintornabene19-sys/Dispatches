The Paradox of Attrition: Psychological Defeat by Material Means

"Military theory has grappled for centuries with how to categorize attritional warfare. Clausewitz contrasted protracted wars of exhaustion with those won through culminating battles of annihilation. For the maneuver warfare school, annihilation is merely attrition on a compressed time horizon... I maintain that the most important distinction is decisiveness: when force is employed not merely to erode the enemy's strength, but to create windows of opportunity that are subsequently exploited.

Discussion that opposes attrition to maneuver usually contrasts the material focus of the former with the moral and psychological aspects of the latter. Yet when it comes to the actual mechanism of victory, this is often reversed: attrition wins wars through its psychological effects, whereas more decisive means succeed by inalterably changing the material facts on the ground."

— The Bazaar of War · Read › [more · less · never again]

9/11 and the Death of American Liberalism

"9/11 changed American politics forever. The founding compromise of the United States was the demand for more freedom at the expense of less security, which was then provided by King George's armed forces stationed and occupying the thirteen colonies. Patrick Henry's infamous quote dramatized this compromise: 'Give me liberty or give me death!'

After America's vulnerability was exposed on September 11, 2001, in an act of catastrophic terrorism, there was a call for a new compromise which demanded more security for less freedom. This compromise led to the declaration of a state of national emergency, codified by President George W. A Day of Training

"The Front needs well-trained soldiers, men able, in terms of attitude as well knowledge, to handle any challenge that fortune sends their way. Thus, we have adopted a new approach to instruction. Practice. Practice often. Practice always. Practice near the Front... Both instructors and students reside in shelters they improvised from local materials. In this way, candidates for the rank of sergeant (Unteroffizier) live as they would at the Front.

Spending most of their training time in the field makes our young soldiers comfortable with the rough conditions of life on active service. The student quickly learns the art of moving without being seen or heard. He learns to live without central heating and electric light... Rather than demanding that weapons, gas masks, and helmets gleam, the first sergeant (Hauptfeldwebel) ensures that all equipment is properly camouflaged. Once the duty day is done, the sergeant instructors pay attention to their students, because they live with them under a home-made roof and the town lies far away."
