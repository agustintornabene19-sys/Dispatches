Agincourt and the Illusory Universality of Military Art

Yesterday was St. Crispin's Day, the anniversary of Henry V's victory at Agincourt. As with many medieval battles, the reasons for its outcome are poorly understood. The answer is nowhere near as simple as "longbows" or haughty French knights impatient for glory: the specific cause of the overwhelming English victory in 1415 is very nearly unique in the annals of warfare. This upsets our notions of the universality of military art.

Tactics are less an evolving science of accumulated knowledge than a treasury of methods that are constantly rediscovered in new circumstances. Humans intuitively stumble upon the same patterns in the course of improvisation, and even animals demonstrate sound tactical principles. Just about any battlefield victory can therefore be described using a common tactical vocabulary. These patterns extend across time and scale. An envelopment can collapse a line of battle just as well when fighting with spears and swords as with ranged weapons, even if the specific mechanism varies... It is therefore striking to stumble across something truly sui generis. Only the similarly-misunderstood Cannae comes close—even the other famous English longbow victories look nothing alike—showing how technology and circumstance can converge to create something that genuinely defies previous experience.

— The Bazaar of War · Read › [more · less · never again]

From the Branch

Tanks in Winter

In winter, reconnaissance units must determine the ability of snow and ice to bear weight. Thus, they must begin the study of a potential route much earlier than they would in other seasons. In winter, a reconnaissance patrol can study a route that runs for eight to ten kilometers (five to six miles)... Messengers traveling during blizzards, at night, and in woods take twice as long to reach their destinations. Thus, a reconnaissance unit operating in such conditions needs twice as much time to explore a given route.

A reconnaissance unit operating in winter requires reinforcement: infantrymen and engineers who explore important pieces of ground along the route. Sometimes these soldiers ride on top of tanks. At other times, they travel by ski, either on their own power or dragged behind tanks... Soldiers should erase the tracks of friendly troops. At the same time, they should look for tracks left by the enemy. In winter, the column of march of a formation takes up more space than an organization of the same type needs in summer. Because of this, units employed to protect the column must be strong enough to fight independently.

— Bruce Ivar Gudmundsson, The Tactical Notebook · Read › [more · less · never again]
