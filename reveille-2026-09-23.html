What wealth taxes would do to founder-owned firms

"Nobody knows what In-N-Out Burger is worth. In 75 years, the family that owns it has never sold a share or taken outside capital, which means there are no reference transactions to value the company against. The company refuses to franchise, sells a menu of roughly four items, pays its associates far above its competitors, prints red Bible verses on its cups, and has spent decades resisting every trend that has hollowed out the American fast food industry. In-N-Out can do all of this for one reason: because it answers to exactly one person, Lynsi Snyder, granddaughter of the founders, who owns it outright.

Then, last July, Lynsi Snyder announced she was moving her family to Tennessee and had decided to establish a second headquarters for In-N-Out there. In explaining the move, she told a podcast host that 'there's a lot of great things about California, but raising a family is not easy here.' Three months later, the initiative that would become Proposition 40 was filed with the California Attorney General.

This November, California will vote on Proposition 40, which would impose a one-time tax of up to five percent on the wealth of the state's billionaire residents."

— Christian Angelopoulos, Palladium Magazine · Read › [more · less · never again]

Beirut revives Arafat's "pretend peace" playbook

"By signing the Trilateral Framework agreement while failing to disarm Hezbollah, Lebanon has adopted the late Palestinian leader Yasser Arafat's 'pretend peace' playbook. In this Oslo Model, a defeated regime seeks Washington's favor by signing a preliminary peace pact. Under this strategy, Lebanon's leadership claims it is merely calling Israel's bluff, arguing that Jerusalem is too malicious to ever want true coexistence. Beirut then fails to fulfill its core obligation to dismantle rogue militias. When Israel responds in self-defense, Beirut blames Jerusalem for the failure and demands international pressure against its neighbor. The cycle repeats until Lebanon's credibility on the world stage is entirely exhausted, much like the boy who cried wolf.

This strategy of theatrical diplomacy and strategic paralysis is a direct inheritance from Arafat, who decades ago mastered the art of using peace processes not as vehicles for reconciliation, but as shields against accountability. By engaging in high-profile summits and speaking the language of Western diplomacy, Arafat secured international legitimacy and financial aid while maintaining tactical flexibility. For him, a signed document was never a binding destination but a temporary pause to regroup, leaving violent factions unhindered under the guise of avoiding civil war."

— Hussain Abdul-Hussain, FDD / This Is Beirut · Read ›
