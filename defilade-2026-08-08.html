From the Field
Maneuver in the Mountains: Opening Moves of the Chickamauga Campaign [more · less · never again]
The Bazaar of War · ~12 min
"In August 1863, the focus of both sides in the American Civil War shifted to a small corner of southeastern Tennessee. The region had already seen some fighting that summer during the Tullahoma Campaign, where in the span of two weeks, William Rosecrans' Army of the Cumberland drove Braxton Bragg's Army of Tennessee out of Middle Tennessee at virtually no cost. This campaign, which concluded on 4 July, rounded out the Union victories at Gettysburg and Vicksburg. Although less decisive than those other two — Bragg escaped with his army intact — it put Rosecrans in a position to move on his major strategic objective: Chattanooga. This small city on the Tennessee River was the most important rail hub in the Confederacy. From it, one could travel northeast to Virginia, west to the Mississippi, and southeast to the Deep South. Capturing it would disrupt the South's internal traffic and open the road to Atlanta, offering the chance to split the Confederacy in two.

The campaign for Chattanooga got underway in late August. After several weeks of maneuvering, the two armies met on 19 September at Chickamauga Creek in a colossal battle that gave its name to the entire campaign. That two-day engagement — the second-bloodiest of the Civil War — stands with Antietam, Shiloh, and Gettysburg as a byword for the brutality of the war. Following a shocking Union defeat, Rosecrans retreated into Chattanooga, where the Army of the Cumberland was besieged for the next two months."

How the CIA and MI6 Learned the Hard Way That Covert Action Is Hard [more · less · never again]
SpyTalk · on Stephen Long's The Albania Operation · ~10 min
"Seldom do we get as detailed an account of a covert action as we do in this compelling new book on CIA and MI6 operations against communist Albania during the early years of the Cold War. It is not a pretty picture. Throughout the book, author Stephen Long more than justifies his claim that "the Albania operation was a bitter and wasteful encounter for all the parties involved." The story is fascinating to read in much the same way that it is hard to look away from a car wreck.

For years, Long, a professor of International Studies at Xi'an-Jiaotang-Liverpool University in China, has been a trailblazer with his carefully revisionist research into the CIA and MI6 operations in Albania (codenamed BGFIEND and VALUABLE, respectively). His work has been greatly enabled by the discovery of a large quantity of CIA records about BGFIEND and his use of two substantial troves of interviews with participants done by others in the 1980s and 1990s. The two main myths he tackles in this culmination of his work are that these were failed attempts to overthrow the communist regime of Enver Hoxha, then in the Soviet orbit, and that those efforts were doomed by Kim Philby, a Soviet mole within MI6 and the most famous member of the Cambridge Five.

With regard to the first myth, Long tells us that the British launched VALUABLE in 1949 with no thought to regime change, merely as a response to Tirana's support for communist insurgents in Greece. By contrast, the Office of Policy Coordination (OPC), the covert action component of the newly established CIA, did want an operation to "pilot…covert action capabilities and the strategic viability of a rollback effort against communism in Eastern Europe.""
