A Soviet Handbook for Fighting Totalitarianism [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Jacob Howland

Early on in To Build a Castle , Bukovsky describes the education by suffering of a newborn "future Soviet man," who wouldn't be "sucking away so peacefully on his pacifier were it not for the Party's tireless efforts on his behalf. But it won't be long now before his debts are called in." His path to enlightenment runs from his first words— Mama , Papa , Lenin —through school and into adulthood, where his wages don't cover expenses, food shortages are constant, and lines are interminable. The roof of his apartment house has been leaking for years, and his complaints and petitions have achieved nothing. Infuriated, he makes speeches at meetings, distributes leaflets, and even enlists foreigners to send messages to the United Nations. But then his boss takes him to see an official who exudes "a whiff of cryptlike dankness, putrefaction, and despair." At a general meeting of the workers' collective, he endures a struggle session, expresses repentance, and promises to reform. Having learned that "no man can flay a stone," he settles into the irreversible lethargy of the Soviet man, who has "neither honor nor pride, nor a sense of personal responsibility." This parable is a brilliant foil for Bukovsky's story of how he became an adamantly, anti-Soviet man.
