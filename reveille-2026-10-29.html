Saying "Please" and "Thank You" to LLMs

"In recent days I've ended up discussing LLMs with several intelligent, high-flying professionals, working in roles where LLM use is a matter of routine. They all told me, independently, that on principle they refuse to say 'please' and 'thank you' to the models with which they interact. The common theme in their reasoning is that withholding politeness is an important way of maintaining the mental distinction between an LLM and a human. I've been thinking about this ever since. Because I do say 'please' and 'thank you' to LLMs, also on principle, even though I agree that an LLM isn't and never will be conscious... if you start from the view that both life and consciousness derive ultimately from God, that more or less forecloses the possibility of humans being able to create machines that can think. Christian professionals who are expected to use LLMs at work are thus in an odd position. Here is a technology that your paradigm says isn't conscious, won't ever become conscious, and definitely doesn't have a soul. But to get the best results from it, you need to interact with it in a way that closely resembles the way you would interact with a human being."

— Mary Harrington · Read › [more · less · never again]

Corning's American Tradition of Knowledge in Materials Science

"Founded in 1851 in upstate New York, where it remains, Corning is a manufacturer of specialized glass that has a market capitalization of over $100 billion as of October 2026, annual revenues approaching $20 billion, and nearly 70,000 employees worldwide. Corning is the world's leading manufacturer of touchscreen glass, one of the world's leading manufacturers of optical fiber for long-distance telecommunication, the leading U.S. manufacturer of polysilicon for solar panels, and one of two suppliers of glass for extreme ultraviolet (EUV) photomasks needed to make the world's most powerful chips with ASML machines... Corning is a rare jewel of the American Industrial Revolution that has not just remained competitive to the present day but repeatedly enabled technological transformations: it developed the glass for Thomas Edison's first light bulbs in the 1880s, invented optical fiber cable in the 1970s, and developed the 'Gorilla Glass' touchscreens for Apple's iPhones that kicked off the global smartphone revolution in the 2000s."
