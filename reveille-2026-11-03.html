The Long Reads

Britain should decolonize the Commonwealth [more · less · never again]

UnHerd · Mary Harrington

The Commonwealth feels like that tea-trolley: evidence of something clearly once of social significance, whose context has disappeared. Along with that context, so too has much of the detail of how we got from there to here. And there's a link between this fact and the ever more insistent demands that Britain atone for slavery: namely our loss of any account of British history that hasn't been pre-filtered through a post-war internationalist perspective (which is to say, at least until recently, an American perspective). This pre-filter is so habitual now as to be largely invisible. From Mumsnetter to senior politicos, people in Britain have absorbed the idea that the First World War was fought for "democracy" or " freedom ". But no such interpretation was offered for this conflict at the time. Rather, it was understood by the European belligerents as driven by balance-of-power concerns. It was the American President Woodrow Wilson who conjured the notion of these conflicts being contests between abstract goodies and baddies, when he told Congress in 1917 that the world must be "made safe for democracy".

Russia’s war has its novel [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Tom Parfitt

Where is the Russian literature that dissects the rot – the internal corrosion of waging the deadliest war in Europe since 1945 ? A contender has just arrived. In mid-September, the émigré Russian writer Dmitry Glukhovsky released his latest novel , Registratsiya ( Registration) , for free on his Telegram channel, thus evading the censors. It tells the story of a young woman who tries to marry her boyfriend Sasha as he lies in his hospital bed in Moscow, gravely wounded from fighting in Ukraine. Her aim: to receive the generous payments due to the relatives of injured or dead soldiers.

Claremont y el taller del trumpismo contrarrevolucionario [more · less · never again]

El Grand Continent · Ramona Bloj

Una de las historias más fascinantes del conservadurismo estadounidense contemporáneo es la transformación del Claremont Institute. De ser un think tank relativamente desconocido —formado por filósofos y politólogos eruditos dedicados a la defensa de la Constitución—, el instituto se ha convertido en un grupo de activistas al servicio del autoritarismo. El relato de esta transformación, cargada de consecuencias, es importante y adquiere una dimensión especialmente urgente a pocas horas de las elecciones de mitad de mandato. Explica cómo y por qué los fellows del Claremont contribuyeron a forjar la doctrina que se ha apoderado del Partido Republicano. Al derivar hacia un populismo iliberal, el instituto se ha acercado a los nacional-conservadores y a los posliberales, con los que ahora forma el trípode de la Nueva Derecha MAGA. Este conservadurismo de reciente aparición es hoy el corazón y el alma del trumpismo.
