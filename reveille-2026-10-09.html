Back-to-Back Drone Strikes off the Coasts of Romania and Bulgaria Test NATO's Resolve

"Within 24 hours, drones struck commercial ships near two NATO members: a Turkish-owned grain ship near Romania and two vessels in Bulgaria's exclusive economic zone (EEZ). Neither Bucharest nor Sofia has attributed the attacks to Russia, but if Moscow were behind them, the incidents would fit a broader pattern of Russian hybrid warfare: covert, deniable, and disruptive tactics meant to test Western resolve while staying below the threshold of conventional conflict.

On October 5, the Royad Mammadov, carrying corn from Ukraine, sank about 30 nautical miles off Romania, inside its EEZ, killing two crew members. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy blamed Russian drones, but Romanian authorities have not confirmed the cause. The next day, Bulgarian Prime Minister Rumen Radev announced that drones had hit two vessels, marking the first attack on shipping in Bulgaria's EEZ. The Alfa Watan sank with its crew unaccounted for, and the Able caught fire, leaving two sailors seriously injured."

Are Most Stereotypes Negative?

"Stereotypes are a foundational construct in psychological science, often defined as beliefs concerning characteristic group attributes. We present a cognitive-ecological theory of social perception that predicts and explains why such characteristic attributes are likely negative, that is, why most stereotypes are negative. The theory assumes that, cognitively, people characterize groups by attributes that distinguish them from other groups. Ecologically, positive social information is more frequent (i.e., positivity prevalence), and negative social information is more diverse (i.e., negativity diversity). If negative attributes are less frequent and more diverse relative to positive attributes, then they must be more likely to distinguish one group from another. Consequently, the attributes people see as characteristic of a group are likely to be negative."

— Christian Unkelbach, Anne Irena Weitzel & Hans Alves, Psychological Review, via Steve Sailer's Substack · Read › [more · less · never again]
