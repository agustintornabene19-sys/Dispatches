The Culture War

How I Became the Scapegoat in the Jason Arday Affair [more · less · never again]
Nathan Cofnas, Wall Street Journal (via Steve Sailer) · ~5 min
"I have been smeared as a racist and threatened with violence. My university suspended me. All I did was tell the truth." ... I broke the Jason Arday story on Substack on July 21. Arday was hired to great fanfare in 2023 as the youngest black professor in the history of Cambridge University. I showed that he engaged in extensive plagiarism and had fabricated much of his extraordinary life story. His work that wasn't plagiarized was of such low quality that it couldn't justify a position at any university, let alone a chair professorship at Cambridge. Within hours, my article had exploded on X and Substack. Three days later, it was picked up by the Telegraph, then the Times of London, Retraction Watch and all the rest. Finding every lie that Arday told since childhood became a national sport. U.K. newspapers published 249 articles about him in a 22-day period. After initially dismissing Arday's critics as waging a "vile campaign," on Aug. 5, Cambridge announced that it would investigate him. A few hours later, Arday resigned. On Aug. 14, he was found dead of an apparent suicide.

Arday was an adult who was morally responsible for his actions. But he was mostly responding to incentives that were created by other people. His punishment went far beyond what was justified by wrongdoing he may have committed. For me, this story was never about Arday or even Cambridge. It was about the system of DEI — diversity, equity and inclusion — which applies different standards to people based on identity characteristics like skin color.
The Medea of Massachusetts [more · less · never again]
Raw Egg Nationalist, In the Raw · ~12 min
Inside the Plymouth courtroom, there's agreement: Lindsay Clancy killed her three children. What's at issue isn't the basic facts, what Clancy did and didn't do. She's freely admitted to killing Cora (5), Dawson (3) and Callan (8 months) Clancy. Lindsay Clancy doesn't dispute that on the evening of January 24th 2023, after she asked her husband, Patrick, to fetch takeout food and some medication from the local pharmacy, she strangled the children in the basement of the family home in Duxbury, Massachusetts, using a set of exercise bands, then attempted to commit suicide by slitting her wrists and jumping from a second-story window. When her husband returned, he found her outside, still alive, called 911 and then discovered the children downstairs. The call is so harrowing the judge refused to release the audio to the general public.

What's at issue, rather, is a second-order moral question: Was Lindsay Clancy responsible for the killings? Was it murder — deliberate, premeditated — as the prosecution alleges; or did Lindsay suffer from severe postpartum psychosis, auditory command hallucinations — voices in her head telling her to kill — and massive overmedication of prescription drugs, as the defence claims in mitigation? There's compelling evidence on both sides.

Maximum Pressure on Iran
Halting Trade With Iran Won't Help UAE Combat Sanctions Evasion
Max Meizlish, FDD · ~5 min
The United Arab Emirates (UAE) is moving to constrain Iran economically — but it is leaving a major source of regime revenue untouched. On August 18, following a series of high-level discussions with the United States, a UAE Ministry of Foreign Affairs official announced the Emirates has halted "all trade, commercial exchanges, and financial transactions with Iran." For Iran's second-largest trade partner, moving to sever direct commercial and financial ties is a significant step towards constraining the Tehran regime's ability to support its flailing economy. But the UAE's importance to Iran extends beyond direct dealings. Dubai has long served as a hub for opaque shell companies, trading houses, and other intermediaries that help Tehran obscure sanctioned trade and move tens of billions of dollars through the global financial system every year — all without any direct Iranian touchpoints.
Deadline for Iraq's Militias To Disarm Nears but Tehran's Proxies Show They're Above the Law
Bridget Toomey, FDD · ~5 min
Iraq's new prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, appears to be trying to reign in the Iranian-backed militias that have spread terror and disunion in his country. But a recent arrest may point to the limits of his power. Iraq's Counter-Terrorism Service detained Abbas Hussein al-Yaqoubi — an official in both a U.S.-designated terror group and the Popular Mobilization Forces (PMF), an Iraqi security institution dominated by Iran-backed terror groups — but released him within 24 hours. Zaidi set a September 30 deadline for independent armed groups to hand over their weapons to the state, but Tehran and its partners in Iraq are seeking to control any disarmament process. IRGC-Quds Force commander Esmail Qaani traveled to Baghdad to discuss disarmament with militia leaders and Shiite politicians on August 10 — reportedly telling them to hold onto their weapons as leverage with Washington.

From the Field

A Field Tour of Sekigahara: Japan's Greatest Battle [more · less · never again]
The Bazaar of War · ~10 min
I was recently at Sekigahara, site of the climactic 1600 battle that led to Japan's reunification under Tokugawa Ieyasu. This was the largest-ever battle on the Japanese mainland and arguably the most important in the country's history, yet is little more than a name to most Western students of military history. Two of the biggest obstacles for Westerners learning Asian military history are lack of familiarity with the major political events and with the geography. It is for this reason often easiest to start with individual campaigns. The battle was epoch-defining, marking the end of the chaos of the Sengoku (Warring States) Period, while heralding the relative peace of the Tokugawa shogunate, which lasted until 1868.

The broad strokes of the campaign were fairly simple, with a number of skirmishes culminating in a major battle at an important pass. Crucially, the battle site itself is well preserved and accessible. Although not pristine — modern development blocks some of the sightlines from ground level — the battle took place in a bowl that allows one to see what the various commanders were seeing.
Machine Gun Man (III) [more · less · never again]
Bruce Ivar Gudmundsson, The Tactical Notebook · ~7 min
On 27 August 1916, the Kingdom of Romania entered, on the side of France, Russia, Serbia, and the British Empire, the First World War. In response to this surprise, the Central Powers cobbled together a pair of polyglot field forces that, within three weeks of the onset of the crisis, proved able to invade the homeland of their new enemy. From the north, the German Ninth Army, made up of German and Austro-Hungarian forces, crossed the Carpathian Mountains. From the south, Army Group Mackensen (made up of Bulgarian, Austro-Hungarian, and Turkish elements) attacked along the Black Sea Coast. Like other armies that fought on the continent of Europe at the time, the German armies invading Romania were supplied almost entirely by railroad — which presented problems for the Ninth Army, which, having fought its way through the Carpathians, now found those mountains standing between itself and its logistical lifeline.

Bookshelf

The World That Never Was: Liberal Internationalism's Last Crusade [more · less · never again]
American Affairs · on McFaul's Autocrats vs. Democrats · ~12 min
In 1991, Michael McFaul felt that he was on the edge of utopia. Russia was free of communism, and America was free to usher in a new era of liberal internationalist hegemony. The arc of history had never bent so far toward justice. Over thirty years later, those dreams have crashed back to Earth. And in Autocrats vs. Democrats: China, Russia, America, and the New Global Disorder, McFaul seeks to put together how it all went wrong. His answer is unsurprising: an international movement of conservatives, led by Russian President Vladimir Putin, has sought to destroy democracy. With President Donald Trump's election victories, they have nearly succeeded. Hope lies in a vigorous counterattack from the liberal internationalists.

It would be very easy for conservatives and nationalists to dismiss his book as a musty last gasp of the post–Cold War liberal elite. His work might well be the literary equivalent of a Japanese soldier still fighting in 1975. But it should not be ignored.
