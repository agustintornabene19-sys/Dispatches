Thanksgiving [more · less · never again]

Mere Orthodoxy · Mere Orthodoxy

The biggest problem for this framing of the relationship between Christianity and public life in America is simply the fact of how Christianity actually has influenced and shaped America over time. The Christianity that shaped our nation's founding was a Christianity friendly to free speech, to freedom of religion, to a free press, and to freedom of assembly. It was a Christianity that was deeply hostile to arbitrary power and tyranny—though with a tragic gap in its application, for which our nation has been paying ever since (and rightly so, if you follow President Lincoln's argument in the Second Inaugural). It was also a Christianity that, in time, saw fit to encode into our civic life a day set aside for gratitude and liberality and feasting. Thanksgiving is, in this sense, a Christian civic celebration.

«Bukele no sirve a Dios, sino al Anticristo» [more · less · never again]

El Grand Continent · anainesfernandez

Mi primera reacción, por supuesto, fue pensar que Bukele bromeaba, o al menos usaba la ironía, al autodenominarse «hombre de Dios», como cuando se proclama «el dictador más cool del mundo» o «rey-filósofo». Pero enseguida comprendí que la forma en que él interpreta esa frase no tiene ninguna importancia. Lo que importa es cómo funciona en nuestro espacio sociopolítico. En este punto, la respuesta es clara: si actúo como un hombre de Dios, me sitúo por encima de toda ley y de toda restricción legal. Por lo tanto, puedo hacer lo que quiera, utilizar todos los medios a mi alcance, porque Dios mismo actúa a través de mí.

Marc Bloch’s physical and intellectual courage [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Margareta Melén

In the spring of 1943, the French Resistance recruited somebody who was, as it turned out, indispensable. The medieval historian Marc Bloch, a 56-year-old father of six, who, with his silver-grey hair, walking cane and briefcase, was the very image of a distinguished Sorbonne professor until the Vichy regime and its antisemitic legislation forced him into hiding. Before long, he was involved in every aspect of Resistance operations, from passing secrets to young men in sheepskin jackets in the back streets of Lyons – his face as unreadable as when he set essays for his students – to making sure the Rhône-Alpes network was as effective as possible. One of his fellow fighters later said that the Resistance in the region could be split into two phases: before Bloch, when it was all about individual courage and determination, and after Bloch, ‘when there really was an organisation’. As the historian Annette Becker noted in the foreword to L’histoire, la guerre, la résistance , a 2006 collection of his writings, for Bloch the Resistance was a way ‘to continue living by never ceasing to be a historian’.
