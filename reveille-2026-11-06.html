The dawn of the post-Trump era [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Luke A. Nichter

The best guide to what comes next is 1958. Voters were shaken by a recession and uneasy about the Middle East. American marines landed in Lebanon that July. The Democrats romped home. Their House seats grew from 234 to 283, and they gained 15 Senate seats and six governorships. Eisenhower, therefore, spent his last two years holding the line with his veto against a Congress led by Sam Rayburn and Lyndon Johnson. On election night, Trump posted an image of a red cap reading 'VETO'. Eisenhower's personal popularity survived his lame-duck years, but his vision for 'Modern Republicanism' did not. Nixon, his heir, lost in 1960 , and four years later the party's soul had been captured by Barry Goldwater. Trump may be a lame duck; the question is whether MAGA is, too.

Is the Democratic Majority Finally Emerging? [more · less · never again]

Compact · John B. Judis

Does this week's midterm result more closely resemble 1994 or 2006? I think the answer is obvious. The Democrats would have probably picked up seats if a different Republican had been president, but it was Donald Trump's performance in office that triggered the tsunami that has engulfed his party. In 2016 and 2024, Trump ran and won on a program that included securing America's border while deporting those who had committed crimes; using tariffs and tax expenditures to revive American manufacturing; "draining the swamp"; eliminating the excesses of what Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez recently called "Woke 1.0"; ending (not starting) "forever wars"; and "drill baby drill" to push down oil prices. But the law-and-order appeal of Trump's immigration approach has been clouded by ICE's killing of Americans. Instead of a coherent plan to revive manufacturing, we have an arbitrary and erratic tariff regime. Instead of draining the swamp, Trump has overseen the spread of corruption. The list goes on.

Hamilton’s Counter-Revolutionary Republicanism [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Michael Lucchese

Among the most neglected aspects of his legacy, though, is his unflinching opposition to the radicalism of the French Revolution. Although many Americans in the Early Republic initially thought of the uprising as part of an expanding flame of liberty set alight on this side of the Atlantic, Hamilton quickly saw how its rising fire differed from his Founding. Unlike certain European reactionaries, however, he never wavered in his republicanism or commitment to natural rights. Indeed, his arguments from the 1790s show the conservative foundations of the American way of thinking about such concepts. Like his contemporary Edmund Burke or Alexis de Tocqueville a generation later, Hamilton believed that religion and self-government are mutually elevating principles necessary for a free and responsible society.
