Iran’s quest for its own Monroe Doctrine [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Meir Javedanfar

First, it has attacked regional countries that host American military bases on their soil. This is why Iran has targeted all the Arab Gulf states and Jordan during the recent war. Iran hopes that this will force the host countries to view these bases on their soil as a liability, and conclude that US bases attract insecurity and conflict, rather than security and stability. As the speaker of Iran's Parliament, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, stated in May this year: 'some governments in the region believe that the presence of America will bring them security. But recent events [i.e. attacks against US bases in Gulf countries] have illustrated that such a presence does not create security, rather, it creates insecurity for them'.

The World That Will Not Stay [more · less · never again]

Mere Orthodoxy · Mere Orthodoxy

Wanting to be part of something greater than yourself is not the foolishness of youth. All of us want our labor and love to be held together by something more durable than individual achievement. In many ways, the Marine Corps does provide this. The problem comes when people realize that even the purpose and belonging that military service provides does not last. For most Marines, this realization comes as retirement nears. The title of Marine still feels honorable, but it is not as important as it once was. Promotions and awards no longer have the same appeal. And when the career is about to end, the question Cather asked presses hard: What was it all worth? What remains in the end?

Jefferson and America, Right or Wrong [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Peter Onuf & Francis D. Cogliano

Yet Parton's apparently simplistic formulation pivoted on an acute awareness of the uncertain outcome of America's perilous experiment in republican self-government and pointed toward a more nuanced and illuminating understanding of Jefferson's role in America's national narrative. If the federal republic failed in yet another civil war, Jefferson would then, finally and definitively, be proven "wrong." Patriotic exceptionalists might proclaim the new nation's "manifest destiny" or invoke God's providential sanction, but they always knew the future was theirs to make, in their own time. "The earth belongs to the living," Jefferson told fellow Virginian James Madison in 1789. It was a generational trust or legacy that could be as easily betrayed and lost as preserved and passed on to succeeding generations.
