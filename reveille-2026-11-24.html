The Long Reads

Perspectives on Power [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · Brian Pawlowski

In The Return of the Great Powers , Cambridge historian Brendan Simms disabuses us of this view. Drawing on history, he argues that great-power competition never disappeared so much as temporarily receded from view. The "unipolar moment" that followed the Cold War was a historical exception, not a permanent transformation of international affairs. Importantly, for Simms, the return of great powers is not simply a knee-jerk reaction to Russian aggression or China's ambitions. It reflects something more fundamental about the international system. States compete for security, influence, resources, and position. Ambitions collide. Relative strength changes. The resulting order is neither permanent nor self-sustaining. It must be preserved, purposefully adapted, or it falls.

Bond and the curse of the British Empire [more · less · never again]

UnHerd · Mary Harrington

Either way, it's easy enough to read Fleming's early Bond novels, and 007 himself, as an oblique working-through of the author's own bewildered shock, grief, and eventual resignation to the loss of an empire that had, for him, been a formative source of pride and identity . Live and Let Die was written a scant few years after the independence of India and Pakistan, but is set mainly in a Jamaica where the sun still hasn't set on anything Britishly Imperial. When Bond returns to Jamaica in Dr No , written after the humiliation of Suez in 1956, that once-settled imperial order is drifting and threatened. By the time we get to On Her Majesty's Secret Service in 1961, Bond and M reminisce in a tone of melancholy acceptance about "a great breed of officers and seamen that would never be seen again".

The long battle with Russia for the Black Sea [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Dimitar Bechev

What's new is Russia's decision to bring naval warfare to the western shore of the Black Sea. Gerans and surface drones are wreaking havoc on commercial shipping off the coasts of Romania and Bulgaria. Since early October, 13 vessels bound for, leaving or located close to Romanian and Moldovan ports have been hit. Most incidents have taken place in either Romania or Bulgaria's exclusive economic zones (EEZs). Of those, five have sunk (as of 17 November) and at least 34 sailors are either missing or confirmed dead.
