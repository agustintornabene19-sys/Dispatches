The Long Reads

A Victorian Defense of AI [more · less · never again]

Law & Liberty · John O. McGinnis

More than two centuries ago, some of Britain's most influential conservatives criticized the Industrial Revolution. They believed its machines would destroy communities, degrade work, and subordinate people to economic efficiency. Today, a new generation offers similar warnings about artificial intelligence. Are they grasping dangers that today's technology enthusiasts too readily dismiss? Robert Southey, England's poet laureate from 1813 to 1843, worried that factories would destroy industrial workers' independence and moral autonomy. He objected to replacing the organic society encouraged by agriculture with one dominated by impersonal commercial relations. He feared that concentrated urban poverty would foster political radicalism. Thomas Carlyle was concerned about a society organized by machines. He saw society being remade in the image of the machine, structured around calculation and efficiency rather than humane values and imagination. Even education and government would ultimately be designed as machines that value sterile computation. Society would no longer focus on cultivating virtues and ennobling the human spirit.

The rise and fall of the ‘Byzantine’ Empire [more · less · never again]

Engelsberg Ideas · Peter Frankopan

That is ironic, because the Byzantine Empire was not only one of the great political entities in history, but one of its great survivors. It lasted for more than a millennium, a longevity that would suggest it was rather good at developing and maintaining institutions, at coping with crises and at adapting to changing circumstances. Why, then, does the name carry such negative connotations? Why is it that its greatest leaders and figures – such as Justinian and Theodora, the Empress Eirene, Basil the Bulgar-Slayer, Constantine VII Porphyrogennetos, Alexios Komnenos and his daughter Anna Komnene (to name just a handful) – are so little known? What even was the Byzantine Empire? The short answer is that it never existed. What usually gets referred to as the Byzantine Empire was in fact another realm altogether: the Roman Empire. The use of the label Byzantine (or, as some scholars more generously call it, 'the East Roman Empire') is misleading, deceptive and wrong.
